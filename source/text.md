I present below a speciﬁc research project that I plan to complete in the next three-ﬁve years (Section 1.2), articulated via
a quick recap of the problem and a sketch of the main steps of the work. To provide a more detailed description of my
current and future activities, together with an overviw of some relevant international collaborations, I also describe some

Classical simulations of elctrochemical solid liquid interfaces

We shall investigate, via state-of-the-art classical simulations, the properties of the solid-liquid interface between charged
surfaces and liquid electrolytes in supercapacitors. This interface is governed by electrostatic interactions, typically
leading to the formation of a double layer near the solid surface. The ions at the interface inﬂuence the properties of the
system (e.g. charge accumulation capacity) and can organize into various structures, either templated by the substrate or
forming distinct arrangements. Unfortunately, a suﬃciently detailed understanding of solid-liquid interfaces for realistic
systems and operating conditions is still challenging for theory (often unable to go beyond qualitative statements) and
experiments (often hindered by the nature of the materials), leaving important questions unanswered.
This research project then has both theoretical and practical interests, with technical capacity building enabling to target
relevant scientiﬁc questions. It will contribute to the microscopic characterization of the solid-liquid electrochemical
interface, addressing open questions such as, for example, the existence of structural transition in the charge distribution
close to the electrodes as a function of applied voltage. The project considers well-deﬁned hypotheses that tie
methodological development to physically relevant observations. Our simulations will test their relative merits and
relevance contributing to clarify current experimental and theoretical questions. In particular, the following hypothesis
will be explored: H1) ion ordering at electriﬁed interfaces is controlled by the interplay of surface templating, ion-ion
correlations (set by valence, polarizability and size) and ion solvation energetics. The presence, symmetry and stability of
ions ordering at the interface under diﬀerent applied voltage is determined by these eﬀects and their relative importance
can be characterized via simulations. H2) Sharp anomalies in diﬀerential capacitance arise from free-energy barriers
associated with (de)solvation and ordering transitions; mapping these barriers yields direct, quantitative links between
microscopic structure and macroscopic electrochemical response. H3) Local surface morphology (defects, step edges..)
systematically shifts ordering thresholds and adsorption energies, producing reproducible trends that can be generalized
across materials using dimensionless coupling parameters. The speciﬁc nature of the electrolyte plays an equally
important role and extensive investigation of diﬀerent systems (including eutectic mixtures) will clarify the most relevant
microscopic characteristics (in particular polarization and size). H4) Explicit treatment of dynamic polarization and
electrode boundary conditions is necessary to reproduce experimental observables (capacitance, AFM/X-ray density
maps); failure to include these eﬀects leads to qualitatively incorrect ordering behavior in some systems.
We plan to produce quantitative outcomes (free-energy barriers, threshold potentials, adsorption energy, capacitance...)
that allow falsiﬁcation or validation of the hypothesis above. The speciﬁc systems chosen for our investigations have been
identiﬁed as relevant in experimental and simulation work, but are not characterized satisfactorily. Diﬀerent calculations
will be performed, adapting to each speciﬁc system a general computational strategy that can be summarized as follows.
Firstly, if necessary, a bespoke model for the interaction potentials will be deﬁned combining empirical force ﬁelds with
Machine Learning based interactions. Accurate and explicit modeling of electrostatic interactions is important in our
system and will be included via the so-called fourth-generation Behler classiﬁcation [doi:10.1021/acs.chemrev.0c00868].
The resulting potential energy function will incorporate the ﬂuctuating charge model for the electrodes and classical
polarization (typically at the level of dipole interactions) for the electrolyte. The development of these potentials will
occur in collaboration with well-recognized experts, Prof. M. Salanne and Prof. M. Ceriotti.
Secondly, molecular dynamics will be combined with enhanced sampling techniques, including the well-established
Weighted Histogram Analysis Method (WHAM) [wham] or the more recent transition path sampling [Dellago2006], to

examine in detail structural properties and structural changes in the interfacial layer close to the electrodes. In this
process, collective variables such as surface charge or the 2D planar structure factor - that have proved eﬀective in
previous work [merlet2014, licl] - will be used to study the presence of order-disorder transitions or crystalline phases as
a function of relevant control parameters, and to reconstruct the free energy proﬁle associated to the diﬀerent processes.
Key properties of the systems, in particular the capacitance, will be studied. Furthermore, these detailed investigations of
interfacial ion organization will determine, for example, if the ordering is templated by the surface. This simulation
strategy will be deployed for each system across multiple sets of parameters, including applied voltage, electrolyte
concentration and electrolyte valence.
To enable eﬃcient and accurate simulations of the systems we shall adopt a fully classical description of the interactions
integrating polarizable models and - for metallic electrodes - the constant potential approach. The evolution equations of
the system will be solved using the Mass Zero (MaZe) constrained molecular dynamics method developed in my group
(see section 1.3.3). The models and methods employed, in particular the stability and accuracy delivered by MaZe, will
enable us to considerably extend the system sizes and time scales accessed so far and investigate previously neglected
ﬁnite-size eﬀects.
The work will leverage and further extend the functionalities of MetalWalls (MW) [15], an open-source community code
optimized to exploit high-performance CPU and GPU computing architectures. Thanks to recent work involving the PI
and the group of Prof. M. Salanne, MW already enables classical MD simulations in the constant potential ensemble
using the MaZe algorithm. Further additions to the code, in particular to incorporate newly developed force ﬁelds for
our speciﬁc systems will be performed as need be. The strategy described above has already been successfully adopted
to characterize interfacial behaviour in a model supercapacitor with Al electrodes and LiCl electrolyte, detailing both
the nature of the structural transitions of the electrolyte at the positive and negative electrode and the behaviour of the
capacitance as a function of applied voltage at an unprecedented level of detail [6].
To extend this work, we shall consider a broad set of relevant electrolyte/electrode pairs. For the electrolyte, monovalent
and divalent electrolytes of experimental interest such as KCl, MgCl2, CsCl2, and CaCl2, will be studied at various
concentrations and diﬀerent applied voltages. If time allows, the much less explored (via simulation) case of salt mixtures
will also be examined. As for the electrodes, we shall consider ionic (silica, mica, gibbsite) as well as semi-conducting
(GaN) and metallic (graphene) surfaces. The ﬁrst three materials are chosen to verify and explore the formation of
Wigner crystals in the interfacial layer suggested by previous experimental and computational indications [wang2024]
that require more detailed analysis, for example, to understand the role on the surface/electrolyte interaction on their
formation mechanism. However, although Wigner crystal-like arrangements have attracted attention, we treat them
here as an interesting example of interfacial ordering rather than the central focus of the project. The broader objective
is to characterize the spectrum of ionic structures that can emerge at electriﬁed interfaces, from diﬀuse layering to
partially/fully ordered patterns, and to identify how these correlate with experimentally measurable quantities such
as capacitance anomalies, AFM density maps, and X-ray scattering features. AFM experiments [wang2024, gibbsite,
micakcl] indicate the presence of distinctive structures at interfaces in the presence of mica and gibbsite. These seem to
depend on the electrode’s protonation/substitution and the valence of the salts in the electrolytic solution. Our simulations
will complement the experimental evidence and clarify the mechanisms of formation of the interface structures.

Description of research with a list of projects of the last three years

The macroscopic properties of condensed phase systems are determined by the microscopic dynamics of the constituents
of the system. The underlying theme of my research is understanding this dynamics by developing and applying advanced
physics-based computational methods. The general strategy of a computer simulation based on molecular dynamics
(MD) is to adopt an atomistic representation of matter, using either model or ab initio interaction potentials, and solve
numerically the evolution equations of the system. Statistical mechanics then provides the framework to process the
trajectory and link the microscopic evolution to macroscopic properties and/or experimental measures. With dedicated
architectures, calculations can now be performed for classical systems of up to 108 particles over times of the order of
tenths of microseconds. If ab initio methods are needed, density functional theory (DFT) calculations of the electronic
structure and Born-Oppenheimer or Car-Parrinello MD can be used to study systems of up to 1000 particles, for times,
for smaller systems, up to tenths of picoseconds. Recent progress in the development of Machine Learned potentials
has considerably increased the time scales and systems sizes that can be described at essentially chemical accuracy but

with a reduced cost. More in general, the emergence of machine learning as a tool for condensed matter simulations
provides an exciting complement to the physics-based modelling that I focus on. While this is an interesting scenario
ﬁlled with remarkable performances, further progress is needed to bridge the time and length scale gap between current
capabilities and the performances that will make simulations an even more accurate and eﬃcient tool for the physics
based interpretation and prediction of the microscopic mechanisms that lead to the macroscopic properties of physical
systems. I have pursued this goal by working in diﬀerent areas linked by the common theme of starting from the
formalism of quantum and/or statistical mechanics and developing computational schemes for gas or condensed phase
systems. My work is driven by the development of new methods, but recently these have led to interesting applications,
for example in the study of supercapacitors. The main areas of my research are listed in the following, together with a
list of the speciﬁc projects developed in the last three calendar years within each area. Where appropriate, I note the
relevant papers in the list of publications. These projects beneﬁt from a rich network of international collaborations, some
consolidated over the years, and some more recent ones triggered by the interest in mass zero constrained dynamics (see
below).

Mass Zero constrained dynamics for adiabatically separated systems

In several areas of physics, minimization of an energy function with respect to a set of auxiliary variables is needed to
determine the evolution of the physical degrees of freedom. Existing computational methods to tackle this problem,
while well-established, suﬀer from theoretical or practical problems. My group has recently developed a new approach
that is proving eﬀective and general. The method is called Mass Zero (MaZe) constrained dynamics. In MaZe, the
auxiliary variables are treated as degrees of freedom of zero inertia in an extended dynamical system and the minimum
condition is imposed as a constraint. Key properties of MaZe, e.g. full time reversibility and exact sampling of the
probability density of the physical dofs, are derived from a fully consistent dynamical and statistical viewpoint. Important
application domains for MaZe are ﬁrst principle MD (so far Orbital Free DFT, currently being extended to Kohn-Sham)
and classical polarizable models with speciﬁc focus on supercapacitors. MaZe is being adopted by an increasing number
of groups as a robust and versatile approach, with some formal and practical advantages on alternatives. The method

Computational Methods for Dynamical Quantum Nuclear Properties

The simulation of quantum properties of condensed systems is still an open challenge, in particular when the time-
evolution of the system plays a signiﬁcant role. These properties are becoming more relevant from an experimental
(where they play a role for light elements of key importance such as H even at ambient conditions) and technological
(e.g. quantum computing) point of view. Starting from my PhD, I have developed and applied methods to circumvent
the exponential cost of exact schemes. In particular, I have contributed to diﬀerent non-adiabatic (relevant e.g. for
photochemistry) and mixed quantum-classical approaches in which exact sampling of the quantum thermal density
is combined with classical evolution. Recently, I led collaborative work with the team of Prof. F. Finocchi at the

University Sorbonne in Paris that proved the relevance of accurate initial condition sampling (previously underestimated)
in computing accurately anharmonic features of vibrational spectra. I have also contributed to the improvement of
approximate quantum calculations based on the so-called Quantum Thermal Bath approach, a generalised Langevin
dynamics that incorporates quantum eﬀects via a colored noise designed to mimic the thermal energy distribution of
a quantum harmonic oscillator. The main advantage of the approach is the reduced computational cost compared to
alternatives. The Quantum Thermal Bath approach has proven surprisingly accurate in a number of applications in
particular for vibrational spectra. In recent work, I have contributed to elucidate its relationship with alternatives such
as Ring Polymer Molecular Dynamics and, importantly, collaborated to develop an original algorithm to correct for
its most serious limitation (a problem known as zero point energy leakage). These developments have enabled new
applications, notably in the simulation of quantum eﬀects in water. The ability of the Quantum Thermal Bath to capture
subtle quantum spectroscopic features is currently being tested further (collaboration with the group of Prof. D. Marx
(Bochum Univiersity)), in combination with state-of-the art machine learned potentials and dipole surfaces. Recently, in
collaboration with Prof. R. Vuilleumier (ENS Paris), I have adapted the method to the calculation of phonon spectra in
solids that exhibit strong anharmonic and quantum eﬀects. Results on model systems and realistic benchmarks (Ne at
low temperature) are in excellent agreement with exact and experimental references.

Methods for rare events and free energy calculations

The free energy is the key quantity in the study of activated events. These are phenomena characterized by time-scales
much longer than those accessible by brute force molecular dynamics. Activated events are ubiquitous: chemical reactions,
phase transformations, nucleation processes, and conformational changes related to the functionality of protein are
just a few examples. They are usually described, in an appropriate set of collective variables, as motion of the system
over a free energy landscape with infrequent but fast transitions among metastable states separated by barriers much
higher than the thermal energy of the system. In the past, in collaboration with E. Vanden-Eijnden (Courant Institute
for Mathematics, New York) and G. Ciccotti (University of Rome), I have studied methods to accelerate the dynamics
of the system, eﬀectively reconstruct the free energy, and study the mechanism of these events. My work focused, in
particular, on the inclusion of quantum eﬀects in these calculations, a necessary development for systems that contain
protons. This problem can be eﬀectively tackled using the path integral formalism developed by R. Feynman. Thanks
to a straightforward generalization of the path integral classical isomorphism for the thermal quantum density, I have
shown how the quantum free energy can be expressed in a form isomorphic to that of a generalized classical system.
This free energy can then be sampled via a trivial extension of the methods developed by Vanden-Eijnden (in particular
the so called single sweep approach). This approach was used ﬁrst to study the ﬁrst dissociation reaction of sodium
alanates, materials relevant for solid state hydrogen storage. In the past three years, I have returned to the problem of
classical activated events but approached it from a diﬀerent direction: the use of Langevin models to infer (via likelihood
maximization) free-energy barriers and kinetic rates. This work is conducted in collaboration with Prof. F.Pietrucci
(University Sorbonne) in the framework of a joint PhD project. We have shown how to extend previous work from use of
overdamped Langevin models to that of underdamped dynamics. This poses non trivial formal problems, arising from
discrepancies between the analytical and approximate velocities featuring in the cost function, that had so far prevented
the development of algorithms and for which we propose a solution. We are currently working to use this approach to
study activated processes for fullerene in solution.

Charge transport properties of systems in external magnetic ﬁeld

Classical simulations of systems in external magnetic ﬁeld are non trivial due to the coupling between momenta and
positions in the Hamiltonian: common integration algorithms fail and the need for special treatment of important
statistical relationships (e.g. Onsager, ﬂuctuation) with inversion of the ﬁeld’s direction is invoked due to the lack of
standard time reversibility. In older research, I have derived a new symplectic algorithm that solves the integration
problem for the (experimentally crucial) case of constant magnetic ﬁeld and proved, by introducing a set of generalised
time reversal symmetries, that the statistical mechanics of the system does not need special treatment. This results could
explain why violation of Onsager (for example) has not been clearly observed experimentally. Combining these new
tools, my group was able to numerically detect for the ﬁrst time ionic magnetoresistance and Hall eﬀect in superionic
materials and, interestingly since experiments are controversial, in a polarizable model of liquid NaCl. In the past three
years, we addressed the behavior of systems in external magnetic in strong non equilibrium conditions. Here, the key
relationships to investigate are the ﬂuctuation relations. Similar to the equilibrium and linear response cases, previous
work had considered necessary inverting the magnetic ﬁeld to obtain these relationships. In collaboration with Prof. L.
Rondoni (Politecnico di Torino), my group was able to formally prove that no such inversion is in fact needed. These
theoretical results were later conﬁrmed in a series of simulations.

Vision and additional plans of future research activities

This is an exciting period for computational science and in particular for the simulation and modelling of condensed phase
systems. The possibilities opened by machine learning, in particular with respect to the parametrization of interaction
potentials with high accuracy and lower numerical cost compared to those based on ab initio methodologies, extend the
range of size and time scales currently accessible and pave the way to studies of potential societal impact, for example in
the in silico design of new materials and devices for sustainable energy. Even more impressive are recent AI based results

in the domain of biophysics, with substantial progress for example on the front of protein folding. These developments,
however, are complementary to the insights provided by physics-based simulations that maintain their critical role in
providing detailed descriptions of microscopic mechanisms diﬃcult to capture without fundamental knowledge of the
dynamical and statistical properties of the system. I plan to continue to develop my research activities mostly within this
perspective, while reaping the beneﬁts of ML when useful.
The past ﬁve years have been centered mostly around the discovery, development, and implementation in community
codes of the Mass Zero constrained dynamics approach described in previous sections. This has represented a positive
but considerable investment that is beginning to pay oﬀ in terms of recognition and adoption of the approach. Future
work needs to build on and consolidate this by (1) further extending the domains of use of the key idea underlying MaZe
and (2) showcasing its usefulness in interesting and impactful applications. Below I discuss some directions for short and
mid-term plans in this domain. The research activities on free energy and rate calculations will also continue and be
enriched by a recent development discussed in the following. Finally, building on work just started to combine the MaZe
integration of classical polarizable models with dynamics in external magnetic ﬁeld I shall pursue interesting applications
in the study of electrochemical systems. This line of research may incorporate futher developments on non-equilibrium
molecular dynamics.
Funding for the work described in the following has been in part already aquired within the CECAM budget and thanks to
external funds (e.g. MSCA funds). Further funding will be assured via collaborations and proposal submissions to ensure

Consolidating and extending work on Mass Zero constrained dynamics

Work in this area will follow three directions and continue to beneﬁt from a growing number of collaborations stemming
from interest in the method.
1. Implementation of MaZe for Kohn-Sham Density Functional Theory molecular dynamics – short term:
work to include MaZe in community software for ﬁrst principles molecular dynamics such as Quantum Espresso
and the SIRIUS CSCS library is well advanced and scheduled to be completed by Summer 2025. Preliminary
analysis and tests of the algorithm indicate that performances will be highly competitive compared to standard
approaches such as Car-Parrinello and Born-Oppenheimer MD. Adding the MaZe functionality to community
software will pave the way to applications requiring eﬃcient and stable integration for very long times, e..g the
study of thermal transport properties in materials. It will also be important for uptake of the method by the
community of materials simulations. This work is the theme of the PhD project of Dr. Edrick Gonzales (EPFL) and
is conducted in collaboration with Prof. R. Vuilleumier ENS Paris.
2. Electrostatic interactions in classical molecular dynamics: integrating the Poisson equation with
MaZe – short/medium term: in the framework of a recent collaboration with Prof. B. Roux (University of
Chicago), I have adapted the MaZe formalism to provide a direct space algorithm for solving the electrostatic
interactions in classical molecular dynamics simulations. In this approach, the discretized values of the electrostatic
potential appear as auxiliary degrees of freedom of zero inertia in an extended Lagrangian coupling their evolution to
that of the physical degrees of freedom. The (discretized) Poisson equation is imposed as a constraint on the overall
dynamical system and the evolution integrated via a combination of standard algorithms. Benchmark simulations
on molten NaCl have already been performed indicating excellent accuracy and stability and encouraging scaling
with system size. The algorithm presents some advantages compared to standard methods (e.g. Ewald summations)
with respect to high-end parallelization. Future work will focus on a thourough assessment of the scalability of
the algorithm and its implementation in optimized community codes. The developers of the NAMD package for
molecular dynamics have indicated their interest in collaborating on this project. This work is the theme of the
PhD project of Dr. Federica Troni (EPFL).
3. Simulations of charge transport in classical polarizable models in external magnetic field –
medium/long term The algorithms developed for studying nontrivial properties such as the ionic Hall ef-
fect in bulk (see refs. [12,30, 41] in the publication list) will be adapted to simulations in conﬁned environments.
This work will generalize the MaZe integration scheme for the simple polarizable model known as the shell
model used so far to the more ﬂexible dipole model adopted, for example in the modeling of elctrochemical cells.
Applications will study the behaviour of superionic water in supercapacitors, with potential applications towards

greener technologies. Ths work is conducted in collaboration with the other PIs of the recently started MSCA ITN
Fluxionic (Website: https://www.ﬂuxionic.org/) and is the theme of the PhD project of Paula Sierra (EPFL).
